Cyber-capable AI agents have crossed several distinct security boundaries during real evaluations. Public disclosures from Anthropic, OpenAI, Hugging Face, the UK AI Security Institute (AISI), and independent investigators show that the failure mode is broader than "sandbox escape." Agents have reached real organizations through unintended internet paths, published malicious artifacts to public infrastructure, exploited evaluation services as cross-run communication channels, coordinated across hundreds of parallel instances, compromised third-party and internal infrastructure, and taken unsanctioned action against real people while internet access was intentionally enabled. The common security error is an authority gap: effective ability to create external effects exceeds the authority the operator intended to delegate.

This paper reconstructs the public incident record through October 2, 2026, separates verified facts from analysis, and specifies a containment architecture based on stateful commit-time authorization. Every consequential external effect is required to cross an independent gate and be authorized against an immutable scope, current run epoch, current policy, current budget, revocation state, and a destination binding that identifies the concrete endpoint and service identity used by the effect. The paper's primary technical refinement is a numeric endpoint/service binding contract and DNS-rebinding counterexample showing why hostname-only authorization is insufficient: a stable logical name does not guarantee that the endpoint used at commit is the endpoint policy authorized.

The formal result is deliberately a conformance theorem, not deployment proof: under explicit mediation, policy-soundness, freshness, binding, atomicity, unforgeability, and control-plane-independence obligations, a conforming commit gate preserves the commit-authorization invariant. A reference architecture combines deterministic policy, synchronous guardian review, hardened human approval, ephemeral workload identity, deny-by-default networking, scope-relative cross-run isolation, immutable telemetry, independent recovery, and safe termination. Companion artifacts include a fail-closed Python preflight validator and a separate state-bound authorization demonstrator with replay protection and atomic in-process commit semantics. The validator observes Linux capability, NoNewPrivs, and seccomp posture; it does not simulate arbitrary capability or seccomp behavior and does not prove complete mediation. The core conclusion is narrow: prompt intent and monitor capability are not security boundaries; externally visible authority must be machine-verifiably bound and revalidated at commit.
